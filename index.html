For Roger Nelson and Richard Tomlinson,

and for everyone who loved them,

and for everyone else that night touched.

Where I Come From

Crosby

I grew up in Crosby, Minnesota.

The house was basically a two-by-four house, from what I remember. My parents bought it to remodel, and they started remodeling it when I was six months old.

I only have a few memories from back then. One of them is my oldest brother chasing my mom around with a knife, laughing hysterically while she screamed. I also have the strangling memory.

I have two brothers, a half brother and a full brother.

I don’t have a happy memory.

Homeschool

We were homeschooled. By that I mean we were kept in the cellar and forced to work at night. We didn’t do school, not really. Homeschooled meant we didn’t do it at all. I got my high school diploma as an adult.

I did it online, and I cheated my way through it using the internet. I thought getting my diploma would feel good, but I felt nothing. It was disbelief.

The family business

My parents were janitorial cleaners, and they had their own business. When I was eight, they didn’t have babysitters, so they just had us work. From then on, we worked for the family business. That is where the cleaning started for me, and I kept at it for about fourteen years.

We were Jehovah’s Witnesses, so we were around people constantly, in periods. Then we went home, and the other life began.

The grocery store

At the grocery store I stocked shelves mostly, and I cashiered. I hated everything about it.

Why the pipeline

I went for the pipeline job for the money. It paid $25 an hour, at a time when you couldn’t get $9 an hour at a grocery store.

Meeting my wife

I met my wife around 2013 or 2014, when I went to get a dog. I didn’t end up with the dog. That’s about all I know.

Prelude

I started that day the way I started most days: I went to work. I was new at the job, a pipeline fuser. That was an unusual jump. Usually you start as a laborer and work your way up to fuser, but I went straight to fuser. I was learning on the job, because I had failed the test. I was two months in after training.

On the first day of training, we were all in a conference room. I had been up all night drinking sixteen and a half ounces of water every half hour to wash out the drug test that was coming. It worked.

Failing the fuser test made me so angry. I couldn’t do it.

Before the pipeline, I went from job to job for years in the service industry and in customer service. I worked at a grocery store, off and on, for about ten years. The pipeline was the best job I had ever been part of, so I went for it. That is what brought me to St. Cloud.

A fuser is the plumber of the job. On an average day, you wake up earlier than the crew and get to the shop to prepare the materials you need. If you don’t do that, you’re really fucking up.

Then you go around the job site, connecting the pipes that people have dug up, whether it’s the main line, the services, or the houses.

A fuser works with the laborer more than anyone else, apart from the foreman. The foreman works with the fuser too, but he’s always going to be the boss.

The crew was horrible. They acted like my friend in private, and in a group setting I was the butt of every joke.

I was proud that I got to be a fuser. It was a really lofty goal, and I was surprised I was able to do it. It turns out you can do all sorts of things when you stop limiting yourself.

For a few weeks I had been trying to work out how to live sustainably on the pipeline while on the road, so I bought a camper instead of staying in motels.

I didn’t go alone. Early in 2018, my brother Cameron, our nephew Cadell, and I made a plan to get jobs on the pipeline together. We have always called Cadell Buddy. Around March and April, the three of us all hopped in the camper and went down to St. Cloud. We lived in a tiny camper in a Walmart parking lot for a while.

After work that day I went to Fleet Farm in Waite Park, looking for a generator to power it.

I left disappointed that I couldn’t find one for $99, as if that was all I had. I got to the truck angry and in a huff, wanting to get back to where I was staying. I put it in drive. Then I got a text message, so I put it in park, answered it quickly, and put it in drive again. It was from my girlfriend, who is my wife now.

That cost me thirty to forty-five seconds. If I had taken off right away, I would have been about two car lengths farther up the road.

Most likely it would have been me.

Chapter 1: The Events

After I answered the text, I headed toward St. Cloud with Best Buy on my right. I was three cars back from the whole thing.

It was a sunny evening. I had the AC on in my truck and the stereo going, so I didn’t hear anything.

The first thing I remember is the Malibu in the air, out of the corner of my eye. The statement I gave the next day says I saw it swerve a few seconds before the impact. I don’t remember that. I remember the car in the air, and I remember thinking, there’s no way that’s real. Then I heard people running past me, screaming. I didn’t know what to make of it, so I looked up.

From where it ended up, I’d say the Malibu was going at least sixty. The driver probably had the engine floored for three blocks, so it may have been closer to seventy-five. That part is my estimate.

Every lane was stopped. I had planned to drive around it, but there was no way around. I thought about whether I really wanted to get involved. Then I got out.

I walked up nonchalantly. There was a circle of people, a lot of them not getting involved, and I pushed my way through.

As I pushed through the crowd, I was thinking, what the heck is going on here?

The people around me were in disbelief, losing their minds. The ones who surrounded the scene were really quiet.

Everyone was gathered around the Malibu, trying to get the driver out. I thought, they’ve got it covered. So I went to the El Camino.

It was a classic, red with black stripes, in mint condition until a moment before. Now the whole thing was mangled. I didn’t know either man. The driver had become part of the engine. I learned later that he was Richard Tomlinson, who was 61.

The river of blood flowed fifteen feet or so, and I followed it to its head. I stood there and took it in. I appreciated it. I thought it was the greatest thing I had ever experienced. Everyone around me was in terror, and I was in glee and bliss, and I couldn’t understand the discrepancy.

I should have taken pictures. I’ve always wanted to be a photographer, I think, but it never crossed my mind. So I tried to burn the sight into my brain, and I did.

Then I went around to the passenger side. A man was there, head bowed. He wasn’t breathing. His hands stayed where they were. I sat with him for a minute or three. Technically I had no contact with him, because he never spoke back. But I spoke to him. I told him, “I’m really sorry you’re going through this. I hear that death is the greatest trip.”

He was pronounced dead at the hospital. His name was Roger Nelson, and he was 77. The El Camino was his. I believe he was gone long before the hospital.

I realized goodbyes were hard when I understood that he had said goodbye to his family for the last time, and he didn’t know it.

Then I went back up to the Malibu. The flames had started, and people were working the driver’s door. I pushed some of them aside and said, “Let me give it a try.” I pulled that door three times, and it wouldn’t open. It was all crinkled.

I realized the driver’s door was never going to open. The only other option was the passenger side.

For some reason, no one was over there. That side was in mint condition, and the door opened like normal.

It looked like what it was, a car on fire. I thought it was going to explode. That’s why I did it. It smelled like burning plastic.

It was a very small fire, so I didn’t feel any heat.

I thought for a moment about whether I should get in there. Right as I was about to get into the car, I said out loud, fuck it, and I did. I tried my best.

I went in through the passenger door. The driver was very discombobulated, very confused, and he was trying to get out through his own door. I said, “You’ve got to come with me.” He kept resisting. He kept saying he was waiting for his cousin to come get him out. I said, “No. I’m going to get you out of here.”

I grabbed him around the chest, below the armpits, because I didn’t want to break anything. I undid his seatbelt and pulled him out. He screamed, and I thought that was kind of funny.

I walked him to the paramedics. He was in shock. When I set him down, he started wailing, and he looked like he was in agony. I stayed with him while they treated him, trying to help him get through it. There were aerosol cans on the floor of the car. I learned later that he had been intoxicated, and that police found a bottle of dusting cleaner on the floor on his side.

My hands felt normal afterward.

I walked about ten paces back onto the sidewalk and watched the scene, having my own high. I was running on adrenaline, but it never registered as adrenaline. It just felt like a really chill time.

A pedestrian came up to me, a balding older man. He could have been a cosmic entity dressed as a man, because he knew exactly the situation. He spoke to me in a way that didn’t stand on ceremony.

“I saw what you did,” he said. “That was really something.”

“Oh, it was nothing,” I said. “It was just a thing.”

“This is going to stick with you,” he said.

“No, no, it won’t.”

“Yes, it will.”

I thought, no. But it did.

I stayed on the sidewalk until I could leave, which didn’t take long. I got in my truck, and the police directed me into the other lane and around the scene.

Then I went to a thrift store. I was looking for something, but really I was looking to talk to people, to make contact with someone, I think. I was jazzed. I felt so high I was like on some kind of drug, but I was sober. I was just so excited.

That was the most defining thing about that night.

When I close my eyes, I still see the whole thing.

A note on the record. The day after, I gave a statement to the Waite Park police. It and my memory agree on most things. Where they don’t, I have said so. The statement has me going to the Malibu first. In my memory, I went to the El Camino first, and that is the order I have told it in.

What the record says

I did not know any of this that night. I learned it afterward, from news coverage and from the criminal complaint as it was reported. I have put it in my own words.

The crash. A double-fatal crash happened at about 7:15 p.m. on Monday, July 9, 2018, on Division Street at Second Avenue South in Waite Park. A Chevrolet Malibu was heading west at a high rate of speed when it went through the median, struck a sign, crossed into the eastbound lanes the wrong way, and hit a 1970 Chevrolet El Camino head-on.

The men in the El Camino. Roger Nelson, 77, of Clearwater, owned the El Camino. It was a car that had won trophies, and the people who knew him said he loved it. His family described him as a retired truck driver, a father of four, and a grandfather. Richard Tomlinson, 61, of Grantsburg, Wisconsin, was in town to test-drive it because he was thinking of buying it. Tomlinson was driving, and he died at the scene. Nelson was the passenger. He was taken to St. Cloud Hospital, where he died.

The man I sat with was Roger Nelson.

The Malibu’s driver. Charlie Barnett, 28, of St. Cloud, was driving on a revoked license. He had been booked into the Stearns County Jail on July 8 and was released on July 9, the day of the crash, after being cited for a misdemeanor violation of a no-contact order. After the crash he was taken to St. Cloud Hospital with injuries that were not considered life-threatening.

What the complaint says. Officers found an aerosol bottle of dusting cleaner on the floor on the driver’s side of the Malibu. Witnesses said the Malibu was going at a high rate of speed, that the driver did not slow down or try to avoid the crash, and that he was acting strangely afterward, with bloodshot eyes. Officers were told he had a history of inhaling dusting cleaner, and that as recently as a week before, he had been seen doing it and falling over. The complaint also says he was spotted in the Best Buy parking lot just before the crash, and that a man matching his description was seen buying two cans of dusting cleaner.

The outcome. Barnett was charged with two counts of criminal vehicular homicide. He later pleaded guilty to both counts. It was a Norgaard plea, which is when a defendant accepts that the evidence shows guilt even though he has no memory of the incident himself. He was scheduled to be sentenced on January 17 at 2:30 p.m.

Chapter 2: The Aftermath

I never knew how right the pedestrian was. It defined the next five to eight years of my life. Everything changed after that.

The next morning

That night, back where I was staying, my head was pulsing, like there were teeth around it. The next morning it was much worse. It was a headache, and I felt strange. I could tell something was drastically wrong.

After the incident, Cameron, Buddy, and I all stayed in the camper.

My wife was at home when I told her what happened. She was really concerned.

I went to work and told my boss, my foreman, and the crew what had happened. They told me their own stories.

My foreman was surprised that I would do something like that.

I also had to give my statement to the police. I went to the St. Cloud Police Department, which was the wrong one. I only had an hour for lunch, and getting around St. Cloud in that time was hard, so it set me off a little. I got to the Waite Park Police Department late and gave my statement. I have it on CD, and I have it on paper.

Two days after

I had my breakdown two days after.

I had finally gotten a generator. It was so shitty. It was louder inside the camper than outside, it moved around, and it only lasted about an hour. I packed up that night and suffered through it. The next night I couldn’t handle it. I went driving.

I was on a windy road, to nowhere in particular. Everything was super fucked and I had nothing to do. I couldn’t sleep, because of the insomnia and because of the generator. I got to the point where I felt like my head was going to explode, and I was weeping bitterly, and I started screaming as I drove.

Then my vision was catapulted out of my head. It followed my truck about three hundred feet in the air. I watched my own truck driving down the road from above.

After that, everything was different. That was the end of that world.

I made my way into a town near St. Cloud. I have no idea which one. It was probably eleven or twelve at night, and everyone was looking at me as if I could see the hive mind behind the human eyes.

The first year

The first year on the pipeline was really good, up until the incident. Then I could no longer sleep. I had insomnia constantly. I would oversleep for work. It took down the job. I quit about halfway through the year and didn’t get unemployment, and it fucked up the whole winter. It took a toll on my sleep and my relationships.

A night of insomnia meant a lot of pulling my hair.

The winter after I quit was horrible, and very difficult. We were broke all the time. We had to sell a lot of things to survive. It was pretty terrible.

I watched movies and played video games. We sold video games, consoles, TVs, anything we could.

I got worse progressively, but slowly. The next year I got on the pipeline again, and that year I got unemployment. In 2020, my wife and I had our first child. Then I just worked for years.

Going back to the pipeline was peculiar. It threw me off. It was like starting from scratch.

I don’t remember much of anything about the day our first child was born.

We have two children now, and they’re both a little crazy.

The quiet years

I worked at a commercial tire shop for four or five years. I started cleaning professionally when I was eight years old, and I did it for about fourteen years, so at the tire shop I cleaned constantly. I was the neat freak. People didn’t know what to make of me, because I cleaned so excessively.

On a normal day at the tire shop, I did most of the work while everyone else talked. A good day was working alone, being left alone while everyone else talked and I worked.

I wasn’t thinking about the accident. I just moved on. But slowly, through those years, it drove itself back to me.

The tape

Something drove me to get the tape of my statement. I couldn’t ignore it anymore. My brother Cameron went down to St. Cloud with me to get it. I went in and bought it for twenty dollars.

On the drive down, I was excited to hear my own words describe the incident. The walk back to the car was nothing.

Then we stopped at a thrift shop. The others went in, and I stayed behind and listened to the tape twice.

Once I listened to it, something kicked in. That’s when everything changed. That’s when my memory went.

When I listened to the tape, I felt something shift in my mind, for sure. Something happened, and that started it all.

My own voice sounded really concerning.

The second time through, I felt another shift in my mind.

When I heard it, I simulated in my mind what that night would have been like in a different light, with the fun filter removed and only the horror remaining. It was more than my mind could handle. I felt my mind break, and then my memory problems really started.

Leftover panic

I have had dissociative episodes my whole life, since I was a child. When I brought them up at home, the subject got changed. I couldn’t tell you when they started, because it’s like leftover panic or something.

The accident unlocked something. When a trauma is similar to an earlier one, it can open the earlier one up. This one opened a memory from before I could form memories, when I was two or three.

The whole memory is as if I shouldn’t have it. I don’t have a film of it, just still shots. Heavy breathing. Someone about to attack me. Then being chased into a closet at home, and being completely cornered.

What I do have is my body. I’ve always had a really sensitive neck. To this day I hold it, and if it’s touched, it throws me off. I’ve always had panic attacks tied to memories I can’t remember. Those are the kind of early, somatic memories that back each other up.

The fall and the spring

Around the fall of 2023, the episodes got severely worse. By January 2024, the memories were bringing me to the floor. My head would spin, I’d sweat, and I’d get flashes of memory I couldn’t recognize.

On April 19, it culminated, and I got global amnesia. I forgot everything, every single facet. I couldn’t tell what reality was. If I was asleep, I couldn’t tell if I was dreaming. At my lowest point, I tried to end it, because I thought it might wake me up.

I have no idea what I was doing. I had a panic attack, and that’s the last thing I remember. I came to with basically no memory.

The facility

I was in two facilities. I didn’t like being there, because I didn’t want to be. But it grew on me. There were plenty of people, but no one of interest. I don’t mingle with people much. Humans confuse me.

Societally, the earth used to be called Terra, so I am Terrarian.

One scene I remember: a man said something to a nurse in his room, and they B-52’d him. People came running into the room all at once to sedate him. All sorts of people, running into a room suddenly. It was pretty hilarious.

My wife

I think it was pretty rough for her, though I’m not sure what it was like for her, really. We had a few pretty good years. Then everything exploded, because I wasn’t acknowledging the trauma.

The driver

As far as I know, the Malibu’s driver served four and a half years in prison and then got out. Some time ago I messaged him on Facebook to tell him how much he messed up.

Chapter 3: Unscheduled

This chapter is what I do now. It is my experience, not medical advice.

A hard day

On a day when it’s hard to tell what’s real, I stay in the present moment. I go with the flow and don’t do anything too extreme.

I meditate daily. My Insight Timer says I’m at seven hundred and some days straight. I’m constantly meditating. I’m also eating different foods that make me feel less stressed.

My wife helps me through those times. On the hard days, she keeps her distance.

A clean slate

I have to relearn everything. I learn things daily, and I forget things daily. It’s like getting a clean slate every day. But I wouldn’t know that, because I don’t remember it.

I don’t work on the pipeline anymore. I get SSDI because of my memory.

What the bliss meant

People might ask what the glee and bliss at the scene meant. To me it just means I have developmental trauma disorder, or something like it. I’m not sure. I tend to think I might have ASPD, but developmental trauma can mimic it.

There is no script

I want you to take away that everything is not scheduled.

We go through life thinking we know the future. We think our day is going to go a certain way. But it can go totally different and totally off script. That’s the point. There is no script. We think we perceive this internal script, and when things go off the rails, we lose our minds. But there was never a script that you were following.

The future is happening currently.

Each and every moment going forward is unscheduled. I’m in a place I’ve never been, and that isn’t inherently bad. It’s just different. It’s a place, and it can’t be judged as the wrong place or a bad one.

We’re just out here.

If you have been through something like this, I would just say this: don’t pay too much attention to a schedule, because things can go against it very quickly.

Why I don’t say goodbye

I don’t like saying goodbye. Any goodbye could be the last one.

The man in the passenger seat said goodbye to his family for the last time, and he didn’t know it.

So I’d rather leave it with an okay, and then leave.
